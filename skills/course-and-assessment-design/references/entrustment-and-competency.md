# Entrustment, competency, and the evidence base

Depth for `course-and-assessment-design`. Load when choosing how to *report* an outcome, or when a
reviewer pushes on why the design looks the way it does.

## Five-level entrustment scale

Competency-based medical education stopped grading "how well did you perform once" in favour of
**entrustable professional activities (EPAs)**: units of work that may be entrusted to a trainee once
they can execute them without supervision. Progress is recorded as the *level of supervision
required*, not a score (ten Cate, 2013 / 2018).

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Not allowed to practise; observe only |
| 2 | Practise only under **proactive, direct** supervision — supervisor in the room, may step in |
| 3 | Practise under **reactive, on-demand** supervision — immediately available; key findings and decisions double-checked |
| 4 | Practise **unsupervised** (a: with remote monitoring, b: with none) |
| 5 | Allowed to supervise others |

**Why it beats a letter grade when the output is a decision:**

- A grade is a judgement about a past performance. An entrustment level is a statement about what the
  owner will *stop doing* next. "B" answers a question nobody asked; "level 3" answers "do I still
  have to check every output?".
- Competencies are qualities of a person; EPAs are units of work. Capability is per-task: someone can
  be level 4 at one subject and level 2 at another, which a single course letter flattens into a
  misleading average.
- It degrades honestly. A course that taught three fixed subjects cannot establish general
  reliability — so it should license level 3 and explicitly refuse level 4, rather than imply that a
  pass means unsupervised competence.

Record the *earned* level, not the hoped-for one. If the owner still reviews every artifact, the
subject is at level 2 and saying so is the finding.

## Why formative before summative

Two independent bodies of work make the feedback loop the mechanism, not a nicety:

- **Mastery learning** (Bloom): instruction → formative check → corrective feedback → re-check,
  iterated until a criterion is met, rather than fixed time with variable achievement. The iteration
  is *to criterion*, not *for a fixed number of attempts*.
- **Deliberate practice** (Ericsson): the feedback loop is what produces expertise; performance
  without targeted feedback does not, however much of it there is.

Consequence: a design whose first feedback arrives **after** a graded attempt is assessment wearing a
course's clothes. The cheapest correction is usually an ungraded, mechanically-checked dry run before
each graded attempt.

## Why measured targets

Classical studio training measures with calipers and steps back to compare *to scale* before touching
technique — the atelier tradition states the priority of "proportions and gesture" over "additional
details" explicitly. A subject without an instrument (an agent, an automated evaluator, a student with
no ruler) will substitute impression for measurement unless the targets are numeric and checked off
the artifact.

## Why self-grading the course is a conflict

If the same entity sets the bar, supplies the teaching, runs the attempt and issues the grade, then a
wrong rubric and a weak lesson are indistinguishable from a weak subject. Cheap detectors, in order:

1. **Hash-lock the rubric** at sign-off; verify unchanged at grade time.
2. **External examiner** on the graduation assessment only — artifacts plus rubric, blind to the
   lessons. Track the disagreement rate; it measures the trainer, not the trainee.
3. **Owner spot-check** one grade. Highest signal per minute, and the owner's standards are the ones
   that matter.
