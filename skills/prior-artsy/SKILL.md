# Prior Artsy

Use before choosing how to run a process. Deliver a cited menu of distinct mechanisms, not a list of vendors. This is not a patent search or a claim of legal novelty. Use an available search and retrieval tool or a supplied collection of documents. If no sources are accessible, return a search plan rather than inventing findings.

1. **Define the decision.** State the process, population and constraints (scale, budget, operating environment). Specify inclusion criteria before searching. Review authorized internal material first if supplied; never include private content in external queries or a public report without permission.
2. **Gather exemplars at three levels.** Look for operating handbooks or specifications, then inspect artifacts such as templates, configurations and decision records. Use practitioner accounts to test how the documented procedure works in practice. Read the page or thread, not its search snippet. A course outline, for example, does not establish supervised lab hours; a delivery schedule can. Check dates and whether the documented setting matches the question.
3. **Keep an evidence ledger.** For each exemplar record the source URL or supplied document identifier, section or passage, date if known, mechanic and limitation. Separate what a source reports from an estimate or judgment. Where an index links to underlying evidence, read that evidence before calling something absent.
4. **Cluster by what changes in practice.** Two tools that implement the same review gate belong in one row. Separate approaches when they have different ownership, triggers, operating costs or failure conditions. Aim for roughly five to seven directions only if supported; three genuine directions beat six forced ones. For enumerable sources, check pagination or truncation, deduplicate, count, and inspect matches and near-misses before declaring a gap.
5. **Rank for this decision.** Give wins, failures and effort per direction. Define the effort scale (e.g., 1 = local process edit, 3 = integration, 5 = new infrastructure); rank by fit, not ease. Keep monetary cost separate and mark unknown costs as unknown. Mark unsupported ideas as hypotheses, not exemplars. Say “not found in this sample,” not “nobody does this.”
6. **Verify and hand off.** Open the citations for every recommendation-driving claim, check the cited passage and qualify or drop unsupported claims. Name the sample bias (for example, public handbooks favor teams that document their process). Pass the top two or three and the unresolved trade-off to a decision maker or the optional `brainstorm` skill. Update the shortlist and saved recommendation together if constraints change.

## Report shape

Lead with a two-sentence verdict: the options observed and which merits a trial under the stated constraints. Follow with:

| Direction and mechanic | Exemplar and source | Wins when | Fails when | Cost / effort | Rank rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Attach the evidence ledger, sampling limits and the next comparison that could overturn the ranking. Return the report inline unless a destination was agreed; for a saved report, include the topic, scope and research date. Check that every observed direction has a traceable exemplar, that estimates are labeled and that rejected directions do not persist as recommendations.
